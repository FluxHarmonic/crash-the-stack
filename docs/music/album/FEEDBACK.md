# Listening feedback and tracker experiments

Use two complementary passes. First listen to a whole track without editing,
at a comfortable fixed level. Note its identity, emotional arc, memorable
phrases and anything that distracts. Then revisit two or three moments and
record timestamps or short ranges against an exact render version. A phone
listen and a game-session listen answer different questions; label the context.

A useful note describes the audible part and the desired experience. You do
not need to identify the instrument or prescribe a technical fix:

> Obsidian Index, Tension V full game audition, 1:43–2:06, headphones.
> The low answering phrase makes the shift clear, but draws my attention away
> from the glass melody. Keep the added motion; bring that answer back slightly.

Include positive landmarks and passages that should stay unchanged. Separate
"must fix" from "try an alternative" and "keep this". One or two priority
changes per track make comparisons easier. For a first album review, focus on
three tracks per session, then check the sequence as a whole on another day.
Avoid changing listening volume while judging relative balance between tracks.

## Learning by editing

Yes, edit copies. Start with a familiar calm phrase from one tune and save it
under a distinct experiment name before changing anything. Use the exact game
or album score matching what you heard: their section layouts and timestamps
are different. Record the source version with your experiment.

Try these one at a time: mute a channel to identify its role; adjust the volume
of a few notes; change one melody note; move one percussion hit by a row; then
try changing the patch. Keep each experiment small and compare with the original
at similar loudness. An instrument-level edit affects every use of that
instrument; duplicate the instrument if you want a change in only one section.

Early experiments should retain tempo, speed, pattern lengths, order, marks and
loop commands. Some patterns appear several times in an album order: editing
one changes every occurrence unless you duplicate it first. A short loop is
useful for learning, but listen through its entrance and exit afterward.

Bring back the edited .cts, its source version, pattern/order and row numbers,
and a sentence about the intention. A rough musical sketch is sufficient;
cleanup, voice limits, gates, timing and render checks can follow. Preserve the
accepted version until the experiment has been heard and approved.

## Review boundary

Timestamped notes are the fastest route to broad album improvements. Tracker
experiments are particularly useful when you want to demonstrate a phrase or
learn why it works. Neither is a prerequisite for the other. Game auditions
are approved for album use separately; feedback on one does not silently alter
the other or the public listening page.

## Side-by-side auditions

The next refinement pass includes a short before/after composition alongside
both full versions. See [Expression pass](EXPRESSION-PASS.md) for the exact
comparison process and the Relay Ghost timestamp map. Give feedback against
that render version; note whether the preference holds in the full mix as well
as the exposed instrument. A quieter, brighter or more percussive sound is not
automatically a better fit.
